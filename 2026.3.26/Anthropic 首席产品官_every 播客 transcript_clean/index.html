    Every Podcast: Instagram's Cofounder on Why Great Products Are Still Hard to Build

    Dan Shipper × Mike Krieger (Anthropic Labs)

    Dan: Mike, welcome to the show. Dan： Mike，欢迎来到节目。

    Mike: Great to be here. Thanks for having me on. Mike： 很高兴来到这里，谢谢邀请。

    Dan: Great to have you. For people who don't know, you are the co-founder of Instagram and now you are at Anthropic Labs. I've admired your work from afar for a really long time and you're obviously at the forefront of building products in AI. So, thank you for coming on. Dan： 很高兴有你。对不了解你的朋友介绍一下——你是 Instagram 的联合创始人，现在在 Anthropic Labs。我仰慕你的工作很久了，你显然站在 AI 产品建设的最前沿。非常感谢你来。

    Dan: Where should we start? What we were talking about in the pre-production is what has gotten easier and what has gotten harder or stayed the same in product building as the underlying process has changed completely. Tell me about your experience now versus earlier in Anthropic versus Instagram and how you think things are changing. Dan： 我们从哪里开始？我们在录制前聊到的是：随着底层流程彻底改变，产品构建这件事哪些变简单了，哪些变难了或没什么变化。跟我聊聊你现在的体验，对比你在 Anthropic 早期和 Instagram 时期，你觉得事情是怎么在变化的。

    What's gotten easier — and what hasn't

    什么变容易了——什么没有

    Mike: I was doing the thought exercise a couple weeks ago. We know the Instagram story. We had another product called Bourbon. We worked on that for almost a year. It wasn't working. We pivoted. We basically spent 3 months building what became Instagram, launched it, and then scaled it. And asking the question: what is now trivial, and what was actually inherent in that building process that doesn't get easier? Mike： 几周前我做了一个思维练习。大家都知道 Instagram 的故事。我们还有一个叫 Bourbon 的产品，做了将近一年，没做成，然后转型了。我们基本上花了 3 个月建成了后来的 Instagram，发布了，然后做大了。我当时在问：什么现在变得微不足道了，而什么是那个构建过程中本质性的东西，不会因此变得更容易？

    That year, we probably could have hit some of the dead ends we had eventually hit sooner, but there was value in getting there too — like we overcomplicated the product so that we then had to simplify it. I find even the models today are good at adding features. They're not necessarily good about figuring out what to cut out of the product, and that took a lot of just hitting actual real-world usage. 那一年，我们或许可以更快撞上最终还是撞上的那些死胡同，但那个过程本身也是有价值的——比如我们把产品搞得太复杂，然后不得不去简化它。我发现，即使是今天的模型，也擅长加功能，但不一定擅长判断产品里什么该删掉——而那需要大量真实世界的使用经验来告诉你。

    There was something about the process of incrementally adding things. Right now, especially some of the stuff we're building in labs, you can get it to go zero — not just zero to one, but zero to end pretty quickly over the matter of hours — but it's made a lot of decisions along the way. Some of the intuitions you build about what are the right things to put in there, I think you build over time. And so I've been reflecting: ==there haven't been a lot of breakout consumer products even in the age of accelerated AI building. Part of it is because it still takes time to hone your view of what sort of intervention you want to make on the world and then build from there.== 逐步迭代地添加东西，这个过程本身有些什么。现在，尤其是我们在 labs 里做的一些东西，你可以让它从零出发——不只是从零到一，而是在几小时内直接从零到完成——但它在过程中做了很多决策。你对于"什么东西该放进去"的那些直觉，我觉得是需要时间积累的。所以我一直在反思：==即使在 AI 建设加速的时代，真正爆发的消费者产品并没有很多。部分原因是，你仍然需要时间去磨砺自己对"我想对世界做什么干预"的判断，然后再从那里出发去建产品。==

    Now the actual building part, once you know what to build, is of course so much easier. I had Claude basically rebuild Bourbon. It took about two hours. It was feature complete. It added filters which Bourbon didn't have — I think it knew the eventual future of the product so it decided to build that in. But I remember there was a week where Kevin went off and built all the filters for Instagram v1. I went off and built the rest of the app. I would stay up till 4:00 a.m. and then sleep till noon. In that process, you're making so many micro-decisions — how should location work? ==We've got to find a way of accelerating building while still helping people build intuition of those decisions along the way, because otherwise you either get very generic products that are unlikely to break out, or ones that just don't reflect some deeper intuition that you come to about your space or your product.== 当然，一旦你知道要做什么，实际的建造部分现在容易多了。我让 Claude 基本上重建了 Bourbon，花了大概两小时，功能完整，还加上了 Bourbon 当时没有的滤镜——我想它了解产品最终的走向，所以决定把那个也做进去。但我记得有一周，Kevin 去搞定了 Instagram v1 的所有滤镜，我去做了 app 的其他部分。我会熬到凌晨四点，然后睡到中午。在那个过程里，你要做无数个微决策——定位功能要怎么做？==我们需要找到一种方法，在加速建造的同时，还能帮助人们在过程中积累对这些决策的直觉——否则，你要么得到非常平庸、不太可能爆发的产品，要么得到那种不反映你对这个领域或产品的深层直觉的东西。==

    Why vibe coding creates "indoor trees"

    为什么 vibe coding 会长出"室内树"

    Dan: This is making me think of something. If you grow a tree indoors without being exposed to wind, it doesn't get as strong — because as it's growing, it needs all these forces pushing it back and forth in order to make a real tree. And so if you have it indoors without wind, it leans and it gets wobbly and it's not the same thing. Because we've accelerated the pace of development so drastically, what would normally be this incremental thing where you're doing things one at a time and then exposing it to users — you can actually kind of grow an entire tree indoors. And then you have this whole thing that doesn't have the same level of intuition and exposure to experience at each step that creates a great product. Is that right? Dan： 这让我想到一件事。如果你把一棵树种在室内，没有风吹，它就不会长得那么强壮——因为在生长过程中，它需要各种外力来回推它，才能长成一棵真正的树。如果放在室内没有风，它就会歪，变得脆弱，不是同一回事。因为我们把开发速度加快了这么多，原本那种一步一步来、每次都暴露给用户的渐进式过程——现在你实际上可以直接在室内把整棵树长完。然后你就有了这个庞然大物，但它在每一步都没有经历过那种会造就好产品的直觉积累和现实磨砺。是这个意思吗？

    Mike: I love that metaphor. When we were starting Instagram, we were very into Eric Ries and lean startup and the whole YAGNI principle. And I have found — even one of the things I was working on in labs recently — we way overbuilt for V1 before we even got to early access because you can. You're like, "Oh well, we have this option. Why not add this one as well?" That's like a PR of work. And if you get a really good flow in Claude Code, you're firing things off, going to lunch, coming back, the thing is done. And the thing we realized was we'd created this sort of matrix of functionality that was actually quite hard to test and keep up with before launch, or even to explain to people when they arrived. Mike： 我喜欢这个比喻。我们做 Instagram 的时候，非常信奉 Eric Ries 和精益创业，以及 YAGNI（You Ain't Gonna Need It）原则。而我发现——就连最近我在 labs 里做的一个东西——我们在还没到早期访问阶段之前就过度构建了，因为你可以这么做。你会说："哦，我们有这个选项，为什么不顺手加上呢？"那就是一个 PR 的工作量嘛。如果你在 Claude Code 里进入了很好的状态，你发出一堆任务，去吃个午饭，回来发现都完成了。然后我们意识到，我们建了一个功能矩阵，发布前很难测试和维护，甚至很难向新来的用户解释清楚。

    The metaphor somebody else gave me which I really like: it's the difference between getting episode by episode, getting to know characters in a TV show, versus being thrown into the final episode — you're like, "Wait, what are all these things and who are all these people?" Showing somebody the fully formed tree is also kind of a lot all at once. How do you build product these days and still keep it simple? Just because you can doesn't mean it should be in the first version. 还有另一个我很喜欢的比喻：就像追剧和直接跳到大结局的区别——你是一集一集地认识剧中角色，还是直接被扔进最后一集，然后说"等等，这些都是什么，这些人又是谁？"把一棵长好的树直接摆在别人面前，同样是一下子给太多了。在今天这个时代怎么做产品又保持简单？能做不代表就应该放进第一版。

    Dan: I was literally up until 4 a.m. debugging this app that I made on the side at Every called Proof — an agent-native collaborative marketing editor where you can share plans with your team or with other agents. This is my second or third full rewrite, which is really interesting that you can do now. But the first couple iterations, because vibe coding is so fun and so addictive, I just found myself adding more and more — and it created this monstrosity that wasn't that good to use. Dan： 我真的熬到凌晨四点在调试一个我在 Every 做的副项目，叫 Proof——一个 agent-native 的协作营销编辑器，你可以和团队或其他 agent 共享计划文档。这是我第二次或第三次完整重写了，而你现在真的可以这么做，这挺有意思的。但前几次迭代，因为 vibe coding 太好玩太上瘾，我就一直往里加东西——结果建出了一个用起来不太好的庞然大物。

    I got really inspired by another product called Monologue — a really simple speech-to-text app — and how well it works in this age where anyone can make a product. Something super polished and just super good at what it does. So I basically threw out the product and started over with something very simple: just a sharable markdown link. And that just started growing virally inside Every. Then we launched it, it blew up, and I spent all last night not sleeping trying to fix it, being like "I'm too old for this." I've had to really modify my psychology because so much is possible. 我被另一个叫 Monologue 的产品深深启发了——一个非常简单的语音转文字 app——在这个人人都能做产品的时代，它做得极其精良，专注于把一件事做到极致。于是我基本上把产品推倒重来，做了一个非常简单的东西：就一个可以分享的 markdown 链接。然后它就在 Every 内部病毒式传播开来，大家都开始用它。我们发布之后，直接炸了，我昨晚彻夜没睡在修 bug，心里想"我已经老了，搞不动了。"我不得不真的调整自己的心态，因为能做的事太多了。

    Rewrites are now a normal part of the process

    重写，现在是开发流程的常态

    Mike: I remember with Bourbon, our biggest mistake was adding functionality over time rather than deleting it. Eight features doesn't make for a good product — maybe the ninth one will. Instead, it just made for something that felt really complicated. Mike： 我记得做 Bourbon 时，我们最大的错误就是随着时间推移不断添加功能，而不是删减。八个功能不能造就一个好产品——也许第九个也不能。结果只是做出了一个感觉非常复杂的东西。

    Part of how we're dealing with it now is actually being more willing to do rewrites. Classic Fred Brooks Mythical Man Month: you shouldn't rewrite software because all the things imbued in V1, you're going to mess up. There's still a lot of truth to that. But the models can help you diff and see "did you miss anything that was in that first one?" And second, it's no longer talking about a year-long rewrite that might have killed a company — these are like days. So we've had several initiatives, usually pre-launch, where we've built the full-blown thing, realized we overcomplicated or made some core assumption, and then tore it down for V2. It doesn't feel as painful. You're not like, "Oh, a year of building this thing." It's like "Oh, that was last week and now I get to cut out a lot of what was there." 我们现在应对它的一个方式，是真正愿意去做重写。经典的 Fred Brooks《人月神话》告诉你不应该重写软件，因为 V1 里沉淀的东西你会搞乱。这在很大程度上还是对的。但模型可以帮你做 diff，看"你有没有漏掉第一版里的什么东西"。而且，现在说的不再是可能让公司垮掉的一年重写——现在可能就是几天的事。所以我们有好几个项目，通常是发布前，把完整的东西做出来，发现过度设计或者有某个核心假设有问题，然后推倒做 V2。感觉没那么痛苦了。不再是"哦，为这个东西建了一年"，而是"哦，那是上周的事，现在我可以把很多东西删掉了"。

    We're also learning to launch earlier. Take Co-work for example. We'd been noodling on a product of that shape for a long time. Once we decided — let's actually build a V1 that solves the problem in the most minimal way possible — 10 days was a really good push. There are 100 things V1 should or could have had. But it was useful enough to prove something. Developing it for another two months and adding 50 features would not have been more useful. In fact, we probably would have been building an indoor tree, and the second it hit real-world use, it's like "actually nobody wants to do that, they want to do this other piece." The intuitions of the original lean startup ideas are still here — they just manifest at different timescales. 我们也在学着更早发布。就拿 Co-work 来说，我们琢磨那个形态的产品很久了。一旦我们决定——好，我们就做一个用最简方式解决问题的 V1——10 天是一个很好的推进节奏。V1 应该或者可以有的东西有 100 个，但它已经足够有用，能验证一些东西。再花两个月开发、加上 50 个功能，不会更有价值。事实上，我们很可能是在建一棵室内树，等它一碰到真实世界的使用，就会发现"其实没有人想要这个，他们想要的是另一块"。精益创业的那些原始直觉还在——只是在不同的时间尺度上呈现出来。

    What "agent-native" product design actually means

    "Agent-native"产品设计到底是什么意思

    Dan: I've been using the phrase "agent native" a lot. Agents have to be able to use the product the same way any user can — anything a user can do in the app, the agent can do. I basically stole that from you guys. Claude Code is the canonical thing that taught me about how that kind of product can work — it's an agent, it can do anything on your computer that you can do, it's customizable and flexible and extensible. Easy to start, but it can do all sorts of unexpected things that the designers didn't really think about beforehand. How do you think about making products like that? Dan： 我一直很频繁地用"agent native"这个词。Agent 必须能以和任何用户相同的方式使用产品——用户在 app 里能做的，agent 都能做。我基本上是从你们那里借来的这个概念。Claude Code 是让我理解这类产品如何运作的标准案例——它是一个 agent，它能在你电脑上做你能做的任何事，可定制、灵活、可扩展。上手容易，但能做出设计者根本没想到的各种意想不到的事情。你怎么看待做出这样的产品？

    Mike: I think a few threads to pull on. One: a conversation I had with somebody recently — a non-technical person — and they said, "You all are talking about agents and all this stuff. Actually, computers just work now. I always wanted computers to work and they didn't work, and now they do." If you knew the incantations to properly get on the command line and brew install the thing — nobody was going to do that. But now Claude can do it for you, and therefore the computer now feels like a tool that is alongside you. That core insight: it's more than even just adding power and functionality to new software. It's also unlocking the functionality that always should have been there but felt extremely hard for people. Mike： 我觉得有几个维度可以展开。第一：我最近和一个非技术背景的人聊天，他说："你们在讲 agent 啊这些，但说实话，电脑现在能用了。我一直希望电脑好用，但它之前不好用，现在好用了。"如果你知道怎么在命令行里敲那些咒语、brew install 这个那个——没人会去做那些的。但现在 Claude 可以帮你做，所以电脑现在感觉像是一个与你并肩的工具。这个核心洞察是：这不仅仅是给新软件增加能力和功能，也是在解锁那些本来就该有但对大多数人来说感觉极难的功能。

    Comparing our products that do this well versus not — Claude Code does it well. Claude AI still needs to evolve a lot. As an example, I was watching somebody use Claude in a project. They had built an artifact or a new document and said, "Great, can you add this to my project knowledge?" And Claude said, "Yeah, let me tell you the steps to go add it to your project knowledge." It's like — no, that should just be a thing it can do natively. You see a product that was a 2024 product that's been iterated on a lot but still doesn't have baked in from the beginning the idea that every single one of its primitives it should have knowledge about and the ability to modify. Claude Code is the 2025 vintage of that. 对比我们做得好和做得不够好的产品——Claude Code 做得好，Claude AI 还需要大量进化。举个例子：我看到有人在用 Claude 处理一个项目，他们建了一个 artifact 或者新文档，然后说"好，你能把这个加到我的项目知识库里吗？"Claude 回答说："好，我来告诉你怎么手动把它加进去的步骤。"就是——不对，这应该是它直接就能做到的事情。你可以看到一个 2024 年的产品迭代了很多，但从一开始就没有内置这个理念：它的每一个基本元素，它都应该知道，并且有能力去修改。Claude Code 是 2025 年版本的那个理念。

    Even within Claude Code — just teaching Claude Code about Claude Code was a really valuable experience. I loved your write-up on agent native and I wanted it as a skill, so whenever I'm prototyping something it thinks in an agent-native way. So I had it packaged up as a skill. The whole process was: "Hey Claude in Claude Code, can you create a skill for this?" It said "Sure, I'm looking up my skill directory, I'm going to create a skill about it, I'm going to install it." "Great, is that available now or do I need to reload?" "I think you need to restart it. Let me check. Yep, you do." Everything has knowledge about itself, and that unlocks so much capability. One of the things we're really thinking about in labs is: how do you imbue the software that Claude builds to be more Claude-native, so that it even thinks to build in that way to start with? 就连在 Claude Code 内部——教 Claude Code 了解 Claude Code 本身，就是一次非常有价值的体验。我很喜欢你写的 agent native 那篇，我想把它做成一个 skill，这样每次我在原型设计时，它都会以 agent-native 的方式思考。于是我让它把那篇文章打包成 skill。整个过程是：「嘿 Claude Code，你能为这个创建一个 skill 吗？」它说「当然，我正在查找我的 skill 目录，我要为它创建一个 skill，然后安装它。」「好，现在可以用了还是需要重新加载？」「我觉得你需要重启一下，让我确认一下，对，需要的。」一切都对自己有了解，这解锁了非常多的能力。我们在 labs 里真正在思考的一件事是：怎么让 Claude 构建的软件更"Claude-native"，让它从一开始就会以这种方式来构建？

    How teams are structured at Anthropic Labs

    Anthropic Labs 的团队结构是怎样的

    Dan: How has your hiring and team structure changed as the models have gotten better? For one of our products — Spiral — we just hired a new GM who I'd say is lightly technical, but spikes super high on product and writing sense. A year ago we wouldn't have been able to hire someone like that because the coding models weren't good enough. But the downside is the product might not feel as robust if there's not someone who's super technical in all the details. How do you think about who builds products inside the labs team? Dan： 随着模型越来越强，你们的招聘和团队结构发生了什么变化？我们有一个产品叫 Spiral，刚刚招了一个新 GM，我说他是"轻度技术背景"，但在产品感和写作感上极为出色。一年前我们不可能招这样的人，因为那时候编程模型还不够强。但不好的地方是，如果没有一个在细节上极度技术化的人，产品可能感觉没那么扎实。你怎么看待 labs 团队里谁来做产品这个问题？

    Mike: I think you get pulled in two directions but they're both important. There's the primitives and architectural robustness, which I think still needs a sort of senior technical force. I was laughing with somebody — they said "I thought my skills in distributed systems were not going to be useful anymore, but actually those are some of the most useful skills now." I had a long debate with Claude last week around whether the system I was building needed Redis or could get away with just Postgres — and it was a healthy debate where I was only grounded because I'd used a lot of those technologies before. Mike： 我觉得你会被拉向两个方向，但两个都很重要。一是基础架构的稳健性，我认为这仍然需要资深的技术力量。我和一个人笑着聊到——他说"我以为我做分布式系统的技能没用了，但现在反而是最有用的技能之一。"我上周和 Claude 有一场关于"我正在建的系统是否需要 Redis，还是只用 Postgres 就够"的长时间讨论——那是一场很有价值的辩论，而我之所以能有立场，是因为我之前用过很多这些技术。

    Then there's the other side of robustness — have you papered over all the problems with fixes to your system prompt and additional instructions, or have you architected the actual set of tools correctly? Just like you wouldn't patch flakiness in a distributed system by just saying "well just retry it in 5 seconds," you also shouldn't do the same thing with "NEVER EVER in all caps" in your prompt. They're both symptoms of the same thing: is the underlying piece robust or not? 然后是稳健性的另一面——你是用修改 system prompt 和堆砌额外指令来粉饰所有问题，还是真正把工具架构设计对了？就像你不会通过"就再重试 5 秒"来修补分布式系统的不稳定，你也不应该在 prompt 里用全大写"NEVER EVER"来做同样的事。两者都是同一个问题的症状：底层基础到底扎不扎实？

    On the UI and flow side — our designers have moved much more into a split designer-and-builder role. The ones we have are writing and contributing almost as much code as the engineers. We've found this almost co-founder model for some of these labs initiatives — you have the designer who had the original idea, pushing on something, and then the traditional software engineer who paves the trail behind the designer to make sure it actually works. 在 UI 和流程层面——我们的设计师已经大量转向"设计师兼构建者"的双重角色。我们有的设计师写的代码量几乎和工程师一样多。对于 labs 里的一些项目，我们发现了一种接近"联合创始人"的模型——有一个有最初想法的设计师，推动事情前进，然后是一个传统软件工程师在设计师身后铺路，确保它真的能跑起来。

    The best signal for a product bet: breakthrough-walls conviction

    押注一个产品的最佳信号：穿墙而过的信念

    Mike: The one thing that's most important — our gating factor in starting up new projects — is having somebody with extreme conviction. Not necessarily about the exact idea (too much conviction on the exact idea is probably dangerous), but at least in the problem space or the question they're asking. That sort of co-founder or founder level of "I will break through walls until this thing is either proven out or dead." Mike： 最重要的一件事——我们启动新项目的门槛——是要有一个人有极度的信念。不一定是对那个具体想法的信念（对具体想法过度执着可能反而危险），但至少是对那个问题域或者他们在追问的那个问题有信念。那种联创或创始人级别的"我会穿墙而过，直到这件事被证明可行或彻底死透"。

    When we had labs bets that we wound down, often in the postmortem we'd say "nobody on this team actually really thought this was the thing — they were like, yeah, this seems reasonable." That's the death knell for projects. That person can be a designer, it can be a product-minded engineer, it's rarely a pure PM. We actually only have one PM currently for all of labs. 当我们砍掉一些 labs 项目的时候，事后复盘经常会说："团队里没有一个人真正觉得这是那个值得做的东西——他们都只是觉得，嗯，这看起来挺合理的。"那就是项目的死亡判决。那个有信念的人可以是设计师，可以是有产品思维的工程师，很少会是纯 PM。我们目前整个 labs 只有一个 PM。

    We evaluate every project every two weeks and decide whether to double down or release those folks back into the broader labs pool. At any given point, there's probably somebody who can be pulled onto the project that has infrastructural expertise, or has worked with that particular internal system, or has deep prompting expertise — to flow in and out. Nobody's fixed on a project forever. 我们每两周评估一次每个项目，决定是加注还是把那些人释放回更大的 labs 人才池。在任何一个时间点，都可能有人可以被调进项目——有基础架构经验的、熟悉某个内部系统的、有深度 prompting 专长的——可以流进流出。没有人被永久固定在一个项目上。

    A non-obvious finding: scaling teams too quickly is actually a net negative because they end up spending all their time on coordination. It was important that at Instagram there were just two of us — it was hard enough to align the two of us. When the idea can still be held in one person's head, adding more people actually slows the team down. 一个不太显然的发现：团队扩张太快实际上是净负面的，因为他们最后会把所有时间都花在协调上。Instagram 当初只有我们两个人，这很重要——光是让我们两个人对齐就已经够难了。当一个想法还能被一个人装在脑子里的时候，加更多人反而会拖慢团队。
